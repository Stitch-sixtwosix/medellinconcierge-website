How to Choose a Property Manager in Medellín: A Checklist for Owners

11 November 2026 · 7 min read · Medellín Concierge

Handing your apartment to someone else is a big decision, especially if you live abroad and will rarely see the property in person. In Medellín you can choose between a traditional real estate agency (inmobiliaria), an Airbnb co-host, an independent manager or a full-service management company. They are not the same thing, and the right choice depends on how you plan to rent.

This checklist walks you through what to verify before you sign, which questions to ask, and which warning signs to take seriously. It reflects the rules as of November 2026; regulations change, so confirm the details with the relevant authority or a qualified professional before you decide. For the bigger picture, start with our Medellín property management guide.

Step 1: Decide what you actually need managed

Before you compare companies, be clear about the rental model:

  • Long-term lease (arriendo de vivienda). One tenant, usually for a year or more, under Colombia's urban housing lease law, Law 820 of 2003. The manager's job is tenant screening, the contract, rent collection, annual increases and maintenance.
  • Short-term or tourist rental (vivienda turística). Stays of less than 30 days on Airbnb, Booking.com, Expedia or VRBO. Here the manager also handles pricing, guest messages, check-ins, cleaning and the tourism registrations. Our post on whether Airbnb is legal in Medellín explains the RNT, TRA and SIRE in detail.
  • Both, or not sure yet. Some owners start with one model and switch later. A manager that offers both can help you compare honestly.

Many traditional inmobiliarias focus on long-term leases, while many co-hosts focus only on short stays. Ask each candidate which model they actually run day to day.

Step 2: Verify the company is legally set up

These checks are free and take a few minutes. A professional manager should be glad you asked.

  1. Business registration. Colombian companies are registered with their local Chamber of Commerce and appear in the national business registry, RUES. Search the company name or NIT and confirm it is active.
  2. Landlord registration (matrícula de arrendador) for long-term leases. Under Article 28 of Law 820 of 2003, people and companies whose main activity is renting out urban housing, their own or other people's, or brokering leases must register with the municipality. Decree 51 of 2004 (text on Función Pública) adds that they must register in every municipality where they operate and show their registration number when they advertise. In Medellín the process is run by the Alcaldía (official page). Ask for the number; if they also manage in Envigado or Sabaneta, ask about those municipalities too.
  3. National Tourism Registry (RNT) for short-term rentals. Tourist rentals and the companies that operate them must be registered in the RNT, which is run by MinCIT through the chambers of commerce. You can look up a registration on the public RNT search. Check that it is active and current, not just that a number exists: national authorities have filed charges against tourist-rental operators working without a valid RNT (Portafolio).

The Alcaldía de Medellín itself has urged people to check the legality and track record of real estate agencies before handing over money (Minuto30). That advice applies just as much to owners as to tenants.

Step 3: Ask how your money moves

This is where good and bad managers differ most. Ask:

  • Where is rent or booking income deposited, and when is it paid to you? Is it kept separate from the company's own money?
  • What exactly do the fees cover, and what is charged on top (cleaning, repairs, restocking, listing setup, lease renewals)? Ask for the full fee schedule in writing.
  • What reports will you receive? Monthly statements should show every income and expense line so you can check them against bank deposits.
  • Who approves repairs, and up to what amount can the manager spend without asking you?

If you live abroad, also ask how payments reach you and in which currency (see our guide to managing a Colombian property from abroad). For tax questions about rental income, consult a qualified Colombian accountant; a property manager is not a tax advisor.

Step 4: Check operations, not just the sales pitch

A polished website is easy; reliable operations are not. Questions worth asking:

  • Who answers guests or tenants at 2 a.m.? Is there 24/7 coverage, and in which languages?
  • Who cleans and inspects the unit, and how are damages documented between stays or at move-out?
  • How do they work with your building's administration? Many Medellín buildings (propiedad horizontal) have their own rules on tourist stays, visitors and moves. A manager should know your building's reglamento before listing anything.
  • For short stays: how are guests reported? Hosts must report foreign guests to Migración Colombia through SIRE and register stays in the TRA. Ask who does it and how quickly.
  • For long leases: how are tenants screened and what guarantee is used, such as a co-signer or a lease insurance policy?

Step 5: Read the management contract carefully

Before you sign, look for:

  • Term and exit. How long is the contract, how much notice is needed to end it, and are there exit fees?
  • Who owns the listing and the reviews. If you leave, can your Airbnb listing and its history stay with you?
  • Insurance and liability. Who pays if a guest or tenant damages the unit?
  • Scope in writing. Every service you were promised verbally should appear in the contract.

Have a Colombian lawyer review any contract you are unsure about. We can explain how management works in practice, but we do not give legal advice.

Red flags to take seriously

  • No company registration, or a registration that does not match the name on the contract.
  • They cannot give you a matrícula number (long-term) or an active RNT (short-term).
  • Vague fees, or "we'll sort it out later" when you ask about costs.
  • No written monthly statements, or payments to personal accounts.
  • Guaranteed income promises with no explanation of how they were calculated.
  • Pressure to sign the same day.

FAQ

Does a property manager in Medellín need a license?

As of November 2026, there is no single "property manager license", but there are registrations. Companies whose main activity is renting out or brokering urban housing must hold a matrícula de arrendador in each municipality where they operate (Law 820 of 2003 and Decree 51 of 2004), and tourist rentals must be in the RNT. Ask for both numbers and verify them.

How do I check if a Medellín real estate agency is legitimate?

Look up the company in RUES, ask for its matrícula de arrendador number (it should appear in its ads), check any RNT number on the public RNT search, and get the fee schedule and contract in writing before you hand over keys or money.

Should I choose an inmobiliaria or an Airbnb manager?

It depends on your rental model. For a long-term tenant, look for experience with Law 820 leases (see our long-term rental management guide); for short stays, look for RNT, guest reporting and 24/7 operations. If you are undecided, our Medellín property management guide compares both models.
