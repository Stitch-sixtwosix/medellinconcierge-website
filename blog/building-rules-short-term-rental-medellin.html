Building Rules for Short-Term Rentals in Medellín: What to Check in the Reglamento Before You List (2026)

27 November 2026 · 7 min read · Medellín Concierge

Short answer: before you rent out an apartment in a Medellín building or gated complex for stays under 30 nights, the building's bylaws (reglamento de propiedad horizontal) must expressly authorize tourist-home use, and that authorization must be in the registered deed. Bylaws that say nothing, or only say "residential use", are not enough. Then check the guest conditions in the bylaws and house rules, the fines system, and whether the owners' assembly is about to change anything.

This guide is general information as of November 2026, not legal advice. We are not lawyers or tax advisors, so for your specific case consult a Colombian lawyer who works with horizontal property. For the full list of requirements (land use, RNT, TRA, SIRE), start with our guide to Airbnb requirements in Medellín. Here we go deeper into the one document that stops the most deals: the building's bylaws.

Why do the building rules decide whether you can rent short-term?

Because Colombian law says so. Article 34 of Law 1558 of 2012, as amended by Decree-Law 2106 of 2019, requires apartments under the horizontal property regime that are used as tourist homes to have express authorization in the bylaws, on top of the National Tourism Registry (RNT). Decree 1836 of 2021, which regulates tourist-home providers, defines a tourist home as lodging for less than 30 days and asks for the bylaws with that authorization when you register the unit for the RNT.

The rule has been tested in court. In a ruling of 18 March 2021 (case 11001-03-24-000-2010-00402-00), the First Section of the Consejo de Estado, Colombia's highest administrative court, rejected a challenge to the express-authorization requirement and noted that bylaws can limit how private units are used.

In Medellín, the Alcaldía lists "operating without the building's permission" among the breaches that can lead to sanctions (Alcaldía de Medellín), and building permission does not replace the land-use rules of the city's POT (Alcaldía de Medellín).

Items 1 to 3: the authorization and its registration

The key word is "express". Bylaws that don't forbid short stays don't authorize them either. If the building approved an amendment, make sure it was turned into a public deed and registered: amending the bylaws takes 70% of the ownership coefficients under Article 46 of Law 675 of 2001, and the amendment must then be notarized and recorded. Meeting minutes on their own are not enough for the RNT.

Items 4 and 5: the rules your guests live by

An amendment can authorize tourist homes with conditions, such as registering guests at the front desk in advance, occupancy limits, restrictions on some common areas or a damage deposit, as Gerencie explains. These rules shape your day-to-day operation: if the front desk wants guest details 24 hours ahead, your check-in process has to deliver them; if the pool is for residents only, your listing can't offer it. Read the house rules too: quiet hours, move-ins, pets and visitor parking.

Item 6: fines for breaking the bylaws

Article 59 of Law 675 of 2001 lets a building sanction owners who breach non-monetary obligations: publishing the breach, repeated fines while it continues (each up to twice the owner's monthly ordinary fee) and restrictions on non-essential common areas. The assembly imposes them, or the board if the bylaws allow it, and Article 60 requires a procedure that lets the owner respond (Gerencie). Your guests' behavior can end up as fines in your name.

Item 7: fees and charges

Your monthly building fee is set by your ownership coefficient applied to the budget the assembly approves (Law 675 of 2001). If you're told about an extra charge for tourist use, ask for the minutes that approved it and the clause behind it before you put it in your numbers.

Item 8: what's coming next

Under Decree 1836 of 2021, building administrators must report units used as tourist homes to the Vice Ministry of Tourism. Ask which units are reported and whether anything is on the next assembly's agenda.

What if the bylaws don't allow short-term rentals?

You have three routes:

  1. Propose an amendment at the assembly, knowing it needs 70% of the coefficients and is hard to reach in buildings where most owners live on site.
  2. Rent for 30 days or more. From 30 days, the stay is no longer a tourist home under Decree 1836 of 2021. A furnished monthly rental can be the middle option; we compare both in Airbnb or monthly rentals: which suits your Medellín apartment. Still check whether the bylaws set conditions for tenants.
  3. A traditional long-term lease under Law 820 of 2003.

If you are buying for short-term rental income, do this review before you sign the purchase promise (promesa de compraventa), not after.

FAQ

Can a building in Medellín ban Airbnb?
Yes, in effect. The law requires express authorization in the bylaws for tourist homes in horizontal-property buildings, and the Consejo de Estado confirmed in 2021 that bylaws can limit how private units are used. Without that authorization, you can't legally operate a tourist home there.

Are assembly minutes approving short stays enough?
No. The amendment needs 70% of the ownership coefficients, must be put into a public deed and registered. The RNT asks for the bylaws showing the express authorization.

Can I be fined for what my guests do?
Yes. Under Article 59 of Law 675 of 2001, the building can impose repeated fines of up to twice the monthly ordinary fee for breaches of the bylaws, following the procedure the bylaws set.
